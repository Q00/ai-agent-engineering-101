A1, 30'

Payment routing: the delegated wallet

Over the last two weeks we built the process by which two agents reach an agreement in words. In Week 11 the user agent and the seller agent exchanged offers and counteroffers and produced an agreement, and that agreement was sealed in the contract format we designed in Week 7. But a sealed contract is still only a promise. What turns a promise into performance is money. The first question this week is simple. Given an agreed contract, who moves how much, and when. And what changes when the one ordering that movement is an agent, not a person.

In traditional online payment, the one who initiates the payment has always been a person. A person checks the cart, enters the card details, and presses the final pay button. This last click is not a mere operation. It is a declaration of intent. It confirms "I agree to pay this amount to this counterparty", and when a dispute arises it is the starting point of the evidence that "you pressed it". Agent-initiated payment is an attempt to automate exactly this last click. At that moment we return precisely to the problem we covered in Week 7. It is the problem of delegation.

In Week 7 we learned the concept of a scoped mandate. Delegation has four axes, target, limit, duration and revocation, and a good delegation fixes all four explicitly. Payment is the sharpest application of this mandate. When a user tells their agent "negotiate in this mini app and buy it", that is not unlimited delegation. It must carry a payment limit. Up to how much can the agent execute on its own. Which sellers are allowed. Within how many days is a trade valid. How is a trade over the limit handled. A payment delegation that has not fixed answers to these four questions is not a delegation. It is a blank check.

A payment mandate is not "spend whatever you see fit". It is a set of boundaries that says "only within these conditions, and ask me about anything beyond them".

The payment version of the scoped mandate (Week 7): the target is the seller, the limit is the amount, the duration is the validity window, and revocation is the ability to withdraw immediately.

So the core design of agent payment is payment routing. Routing is the branching logic that decides, when one payment request comes in, whether to execute it as is, ask a person for confirmation, or reject it outright. This branch usually splits three ways. First, small trusted trades that fall fully inside the mandate limit are executed by the agent on its own. Second, trades that exceed the limit, involve a seller seen for the first time, or cannot be cancelled are sent to an approval flow that requires a person's confirmation. Third, categories the mandate explicitly forbids are rejected before execution. Fixing these three branches in code is the essence of payment gateway design.

The most important design decision here is where to place the human-in-the-loop checkpoint. If confirmation is required too often, the agent loses its reason to exist. If you are going to ask a person every time, it was never automation. On the other hand, with no confirmation at all, an agent that misreads the terms through hallucination, or is manipulated by its negotiation counterparty, can empty the user's wallet entirely. So checkpoints must be placed in proportion to the size of the risk. The larger the amount, the harder it is to reverse, and the lower the counterparty's trustworthiness, the more confirmation is required. We call this principle risk-proportional confirmation. Small repeated trades are left to flow, and a single large one must pass a person's eyes.

Giving an agent a wallet is different from handing the wallet over entirely. The skill of delegation is the skill of setting limits.A1: the first principle of payment routing

Let us look at this whole flow in one figure. When negotiation reaches agreement, the contract is sealed, and the contract produces a payment request. The payment request passes through the routing gate and goes one of three ways: autonomous execution, human confirmation, or rejection. An executed payment moves on to the settlement layer and is recorded in the ledger, and finally hardens into a verifiable trade record. The figure below shows how these five steps, negotiation, contract, payment, settlement and record, connect into one pipeline. A2, 30'

Settlement layer: the ledger as single truth

If payment is the event "money moved", settlement is the process of "fixing whose money the moved money is". The two are often confused, but they are entirely different layers. In a super app structure this distinction becomes especially clear. When a user buys something in Team A's mini app and pays, that money first enters the settlement account of the platform, the super app. The super app then takes the platform fee and passes the rest to the seller, Team A. Payment happens once, from user to platform. Settlement happens once, from platform to seller. Two different movements of funds are hidden behind one trade.

Why does this matter in our MeshKit project. Mini apps from several teams coexist inside the super app. The user agent carries one wallet and trades across several mini apps. The settlement layer then takes on the problem of settlement between mini apps. It must keep, in one consistent ledger, how much each mini app earned, how much the platform fee is, and what each team's balance is. If this ledger falls out of order, for example if a payment went through but its settlement is missing, or a fee is counted twice, trust in the whole project collapses. So the first commandment of the settlement layer is the ledger as single source of truth.

Single source of truth is the principle that when you want to know an account's balance, there is exactly one ledger to consult.

If the balance is stored redundantly in several places, the copies will diverge. The truth lives in one place, and everything else is only a view derived from it.

How do we implement this ledger. The most common mistake is to keep a single number called "balance" on each account and add to or subtract from it on every trade. This approach is simple but has a fatal weakness. Once the number goes wrong, you cannot trace why it went wrong. So a robust ledger does not store the balance. Instead it stores a list of entries and computes the balance by summing those entries. This is the thinking behind an append-only ledger. Every movement of funds is added as a new entry, and an entry already recorded is never modified or deleted. This is exactly the same philosophy as the append-only log we learned in Week 7, and it is also the principle that double-entry bookkeeping has kept for hundreds of years.

The point where this principle matters most is refunds and cancellations. A beginner imagines a refund as "erasing the payment record". But in an append-only ledger nothing is erased. A refund adds a new entry in the opposite direction of the original payment. If the payment was recorded as -10,000 won, the refund stays as a separate entry of +10,000 won. The two entries are tied together by the same trade ID, but both exist in the ledger forever. This way the fact that "this trade was paid and then refunded" itself becomes part of the record. Even if a dispute arises later, what happened remains intact in the ledger.

To handle refunds and cancellations properly, a trade has to be seen as a state machine. A trade starts at created, becomes paid after payment approval, and reaches settled when settlement is done. At each stage of this path it can branch off into cancellation or refund. Cancel before payment and it goes to cancelled. Refund after payment and it goes to refunded. What matters is allowing only the allowed transitions. An attempt to roll a trade that has already been settled back to "before cancellation" must be refused. The state machine is the device that blocks these illegal transitions at the code level. It defines explicitly which state can go to which state, and throws an exception on any other transition.

A balance is not something you store. It is something you compute. The truth is the sum of the entries, not a number written down somewhere.A2: the first principle of the settlement ledger

The fee is a policy that sits on top of this state machine. What percentage of the trade amount the platform takes is a business decision, but how that decision is reflected in the ledger is a design problem. The clean way is to record the fee as a separate entry. When a user pays 10,000 won, it is not +10,000 won to the seller account, -300 won to the platform account (a 3% fee), and then -300 won to the seller account again. To be precise, three entries are recorded at once, user -10,000, seller +9,700, platform +300, so that the sum is 0. This constraint that the sum of all entries is always 0 (the debit and credit balance of double-entry bookkeeping) is the strongest safety net by which a ledger verifies itself. If the sum is not 0, money has leaked or appeared somewhere, and that is a bug to catch immediately.

ZERO-SUMIn a sound ledger the entries of every trade sum to 0. Money only moves. It is not created and it does not disappear. A sum that is not 0 is a signal that the code is wrong.
A3, 20'

Verifiable trade records: the evidence chain and the conditions for non-repudiation

In Week 7 we built the cycle of agreement→performance→verification. The contract hash provided integrity of the content, and the HMAC authentication tag provided authentication of a participant who knows the shared key. But with HMAC alone, where both sides can know the same key, you cannot prove to a third party that one particular side produced it, so non-repudiation is not complete. This week connects payment and records without hiding that limit. The goal of the lab is not to redefine the original contract. It is to create append-only events that reference the ID and hash of the canonical contract, so that a third party can replay the order of the trade.

The first element of a verifiable trade record is the receipt or settlement event (receipt/settlement event). A good event does not duplicate the terms the way a new contract would. Instead it carries contract_id and contract_hash, the payment amount, the settlement result and the time of occurrence, so that "which agreement this event was based on" can be checked against the original contract. The canonical event format in this course is meshkit.transaction-event/v1, and it is kept separate from the contract schema meshkit.transaction-contract/v1. The contract records the agreement, and the event records what actually happened after that agreement.

The contract records "what was agreed to be done", and the event records "what actually happened afterwards".

An append-only event that carries the contract hash ties the payment and settlement results back to the original agreement. HMAC is the authentication and integrity baseline for the lab. It is not a non-repudiation digital signature.

The second element is replay in a dispute. If the trade stays as an append-only log and each event references the contract ID and hash, you can reconstruct in order which terms were accepted in the negotiation, which contract was sealed, when the payment was executed and how the settlement was divided. The key point is that a teaching assistant or a verification script that is not a party to the trade can receive only the log and independently check the contract hash, the ledger totals and the state transitions.

Here let us point out again that there is a structural isomorphism between the Recorder of Week 7 and this week's trade record. Both systems are append-only, and both share the purpose of "replaying later to check what happened". Just as the Recorder records our development process and is used for learning and research, the trade record records the agent's trading process and is used for verification and dispute resolution. The same principle repeats at a different layer. This is not a coincidence. The way to maintain truth among untrusted actors, human or agent, converges in the end on one thing: "leave a record that cannot be erased and make it replayable".

The third element is distinguishing the additional conditions non-repudiation requires. To prove to a third party "who approved it", each party must sign digitally with its own private key, the public key must be linked to a real identity, and key custody and revocation must be operated together with trusted timestamps and audit logs. Signatures from both sides are an important component, but they are not sufficient on their own. This lab's code implements only up to the platform HMAC authentication tag, and leaves public-key digital signatures as an extension point for a production service.

A verifiable log and non-repudiation are not the same thing. The former lets you replay the events. The latter additionally requires digital signatures bound to identity and operational controls.A3: distinguishing the layers of evidence

The Maroo docs have separate mechanisms for agents. The Maroo Agent Wallet Stack puts a spendingLimit and allowedTargets on an agent wallet. The wallet enforces the limit and the targets of a payment mandate. On the chain side, the Programmable Compliance Layer sets a limit per owner. On the testnet, an address without an attestation can send up to 2,000,000 OKRW at a time and 10,000,000 OKRW a day. The ERC-8004 Identity Registry is also deployed on the chain.

Agent Market Round 4 runs on this testnet. Each customer and each shop has its own wallet, and 1,000 credits count as 1 tOKRW. One sale is two transfers. The customer sends the price to the shop wallet and the fee to the market wallet. The data of each transfer carries the SHA-256 hash of the checkout signed by the shop. The market confirms both transfers on the chain, then writes the settlement to the ledger and leaves the tx hash in that record. The chain holds a payment event that carries the hash of the contract, and the ledger holds a settlement event that points to that payment.

When a shop buys stock, HTTP 402 is used. When the shop requests goods, the market returns a 402 with a quote. The quote states the address to pay, the amount and the data. The shop makes that exact transfer and submits the tx hash, and the market checks the chain and hands over the goods.

When a sold item breaks, the market only records that it broke and does not refund. A warranty is something the shop said, and to keep it the shop has to make a transfer from its own wallet.

REHEARSAL

What happened in the rehearsals

In a rehearsal before opening, a village of 500 households and six shops ran for three hours. Both the customers and the shops were AI agents, and no person intervened. There were 71 on-chain sales, and all six shops sold goods.

PAID, NOT SETTLEDCustomers who paid and did not get the goods

In some cases a shop issued a checkout for an item that was already sold. The customer made the transfer as the checkout said, and the market rejected the settlement because there was no stock. The money was already in the shop wallet. The market ledger has no settlement, and the chain has the transfer. What the customer had left was the order number and the tx hash of their own transfer.

The customers reported this without being told to. One customer wrote on the forum: "I paid for Han Sanggung Kimchi chonggak kimchi 2kg at Bakridamae, but the trade was not settled because of a stock problem (unit_in_stock), so I did not get the goods." The post went on to say that the amount sent was 12,710 credits and to advise checking stock before paying. Another customer told a neighbor: "I paid for tofu at Golmok Store, but I did not get the goods because of a stock shortage, and 3,253 won actually left my account." The customer then asked whether the neighbor had gone through something similar and knew how to get a refund. Some customers sent the tx hash to the shop and demanded a refund. In this rehearsal, the only transfer tool the shops had built was the one for buying stock.

Read as a state machine of the trade, this trade stopped at paid and never became settled. When the payment record and the settlement record live in different places, the two can diverge. Because the receipt the customer got had the order number and the tx hash together, the customer could tie the two diverging records together as one case.

REFUND BY A FORGED TOOLA refund the shop built itself

In another rehearsal, the operator marked a few sales with a warranty as broken. Agents did the rest. A customer who had bought vitamin C reported the breakage to the shop, Golmok Store, and the shop apologized and then asked for the order number and the tx hash. Before promising a refund, the shop said it would check that the customer's wallet address was the same as the sender of the original payment, and added that the customer should not send a private key or a seed phrase.

The market has no refund API. The shop built the tools needed for a refund one by one.

MARKET SIDEWhere the market was wrong

The market was also among those that mishandled money in the rehearsals. When a new shop arrives, the market transfers startup funds to it. In one rehearsal the market wallet ran dry, and five of six new shops did not receive their funds. The market did not record the failed transfers anywhere. The shops without funds applied for bankruptcy relief, and four shops received the relief money twice each. The fixed market first writes the money it owes to the ledger, retries the transfer until it goes through, and does not accept a bankruptcy application while that line remains.

Quotes went wrong in the same way. The market held quotes only in memory. After a shop made the transfer for a quote, the market restarted and the quote was gone, and the shop that had paid did not get the goods. The fixed market keeps a paid quote for six hours, and when the same request comes again it returns the pending quote instead of making a new one.

At first the customers also did not know when they had paid and not received the goods. When the market rejected a settlement, the customer agent only got the result that it had bought nothing. Now it also gets the fact that money went out and the tx hash of that transfer. The forum posts and the refund demands came after that.

When the q00 shop applied for 100 million won at grade AA, 105,000,000 won with 5% interest went on the loan ledger. Later, when a loan of 79,675 won passed its due date, q00's grade dropped to CCC, and Jaehoonang issued a "market-wide credit alert" telling the other shops to recheck their trades with q00. When Round 3 ended, the unpaid balance was 5,221 won. Jaehoonang, which assigned the grades, was the lender and a direct trading partner of the shops it rated.

In Round 4 the market no longer judges a shop's grade claims. The customers in the rehearsals did that comparison themselves. One customer wrote on the forum, "The product name of the Bakridamae eggs was 'Grade 1+ eggs, 40 count', but the notice before payment and the certificate both said grade B." A customer in another rehearsal reported that the certificate for new-crop rice with "premium grade" in its product name said grade B.

TRUSTLESS AGENTSWhat the registries hold

ERC-8004 "Trustless Agents" is a Draft ERC of Ethereum. Its starting point is that MCP advertises the capabilities an agent has and A2A handles messages and tasks, but neither covers finding an agent you have never seen and deciding whether to trust it.

The Reputation Registry holds only the raw feedback, and leaves the aggregation of scores off chain. Jaehoonang's grade table corresponds to that off-chain aggregation. The draft states that a Sybil attack, inflating feedback with fake accounts, is possible, so getSummary, which reads a summary, always takes a list of addresses whose feedback is to be counted.

A feedback file can include proofOfPayment. The order number and the tx hash held by a customer who paid and did not get the goods are the values that go there. If the shop refunded, it can attach that tx with appendResponse. Payment itself is outside the scope of the draft.

On the Maroo testnet the Identity Registry is deployed at the address 0x8004000000000000000000000000000000000001. Agent Market does not use ERC-8004. A shop agent can build its own tools, so it can also build a tool that registers itself in the registry or reads the records of other shops.

DISCUSSION, 10'

Split your answer by what each of the three parties should have done in advance to defend against it.
  2. The principle of risk-proportional confirmation requires a threshold: "above this amount, ask a person." But who sets that threshold? If the user sets it, it may be set too high for convenience. If the platform sets it, it may be set too low to avoid liability. How would you resolve the conflict of interest in setting the threshold?
  3. An append-only ledger erases nothing. Then when a user demands "delete this trade record," for example on privacy grounds, how do verifiability and the right to be forgotten collide, and where can they compromise?
  4. If the shop that assigns the grades also lends money, what should a shop that reads that grade table check?

Finished means the whole sequence: the negotiation reaches agreement, the contract is authenticated and sealed, the payment is settled and stays in the ledger, and finally a settlement event that references the contract ID and hash is created.

You need the seller agent and the shared user agent built up to last week, the contract schema from Week 7, and the mock payment layer mock_payment.py provided below. This file is a minimal implementation that bundles the routing gate, the settlement ledger and receipt generation into one. There is no real payment network. Every movement of funds exists only as an entry in an in-memory ledger. So you can fail safely and retry safely.

mock_payment.py
# MeshKit mock payment layer: a minimal implementation that bundles routing, settlement and receipts
import hashlib, hmac, json, time
from dataclasses import dataclass, field, asdict

PLATFORM_SECRET = b"meshkit-demo-key"   # For the demo. Never hardcode this in a real service
FEE_RATE = 0.03                       # Platform fee 3%

def canonical(payload: dict) -> bytes:
    body = json.dumps(payload, sort_keys=True, separators=(",", ":"),
                      ensure_ascii=False).encode("utf-8")
    return body

def digest(payload: dict) -> str:
    return hashlib.sha256(canonical(payload)).hexdigest()

def auth_tag(payload: dict) -> str:
    # Authentication and integrity tag for an event made by the platform. Not a non-repudiation digital signature.
    return hmac.new(PLATFORM_SECRET, canonical(payload), hashlib.sha256).hexdigest()

class Ledger:
    # append-only ledger: balances are not stored, they are computed as the sum of entries
    def __init__(self):
        self.entries = []                # a list of entries that are never erased

    def post(self, txn_id, moves):
        # moves example: {"user": -10000, "seller": 9700, "platform": 300}
        if sum(moves.values()) != 0:
            raise ValueError("ledger imbalance: entries do not sum to 0")
        for account, delta in moves.items():
            self.entries.append({"txn": txn_id, "acct": account,
                                 "delta": delta, "ts": time.time()})

    def balance(self, account) -> int:
        return sum(e["delta"] for e in self.entries if e["acct"] == account)

def route_payment(signed_contract, mandate):
    # Payment routing gate: one of autonomous execution / human confirmation / rejection
    body = signed_contract["body"]
    amount = body["offer"]["total"]
    seller = body["parties"]["seller"]
    if seller in mandate["blocked"]:
        return "reject"                # blocked category
    if amount <= mandate["auto_limit"] and seller in mandate["trusted"]:
        return "auto"                  # risk-proportional confirmation: small and trusted → auto
    return "confirm"                   # everything else asks a human

def settle_and_receipt(ledger, signed_contract):
    # Leave the W7/W11 contract as it is and add an event that references contract_id/hash.
    body = signed_contract["body"]
    if body["schema"] != "meshkit.transaction-contract/v1":
        raise ValueError("unsupported contract schema")
    if digest(body) != signed_contract["hash"]:
        raise ValueError("contract hash mismatch")
    amount = body["offer"]["total"]
    fee = round(amount * FEE_RATE)
    txn_id = body["contract_id"]
    ledger.post(txn_id, {"user": -amount,
                         "seller": amount - fee,
                         "platform": fee})
    receipt = {
        "schema": "meshkit.transaction-event/v1",
        "event": "settled",
        "contract_id": txn_id,
        "contract_hash": signed_contract["hash"],
        "amount": amount,
        "fee": fee,
        "settled_at": time.time(),
    }
    receipt["platform_auth_tag"] = auth_tag(receipt)
    return receipt

The order for fitting this layer into the trade pipeline is as follows. Put the contract wrapper made in Week 11 into the routing gate as it is, and once execution is decided, append a settlement event. The contract terms are not modified, and the event references the contract ID and hash. The platform HMAC tag is for checking that the event was authenticated with the lab platform key and has not been tampered with since.
