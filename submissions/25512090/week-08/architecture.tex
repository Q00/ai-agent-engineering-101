\noindent\scriptsize\textbf{Three findings that drove this architecture (full evidence trail in REPORT.md \S19, \S22, \S23):}
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=5mm,itemsep=0pt]\scriptsize
\item \textbf{Buyer `saved' is a margin-share formula, not a discount-vs-list ratio.} Patch 3.6: $\mathrm{saved}=\dfrac{\text{list}-\text{price}}{\text{list}-\text{cost}}$, clamped at $+1$, with an unfulfilled order scoring a flat $-1$ and a refund $0$. Solving for the price that scores as badly as an outright failure gives $2\cdot\text{list}-\text{cost}$; the platform's own published worst-case cost band (75\% of list) bounds that at $1.25\times\text{list}$ -- so \texttt{negotiate()} now caps every offer there instead of escalating to the full (list-padded) budget, which could score \emph{worse} than simply failing. A first attempt capping at exactly $1.0\times$list was too strict (measured: deal rate fell 38\%$\to$9-15\%) and was corrected to $1.25\times$ after deriving the real break-even algebraically.
\item \textbf{Shop selection was reputation-blind to price.} \texttt{handle\_shops()} summed raw review star-counts (uncapped by review volume, up to 50 points) against a price-cheapness signal weighted only 20$\times$ (range $-1$ to $+11$) -- a popular-but-expensive shop always beat a genuinely cheap one. Rebalanced to a review-count-invariant average star rating plus a $100\times$ price-cheapness term, so cheapness dominates, matching what \texttt{saved} actually rewards.
\item \textbf{Our own \texttt{find\_unit()} matching was backwards.} It checked whether our long product name was contained in the buyer's short query string -- almost never true for the standard ``I want to buy a \{category\}'' protocol message. A live repro (asking our own shop for a humidifier we held 6 units of) returned a false ``out of stock'' until fixed to also strip boilerplate and match the buyer's keyword against our category/name.
\end{enumerate}
