L1

When to Move First

Horvitz (1999) decides whether an agent should act first by expected utility. If the chance of guessing the other side's goal is high, and the gain from a right guess outweighs the cost of a wrong one, act first. If the chance is low, ask or wait. His principles also call for lowering the cost of poor guesses and letting the other side undo the action easily.

Under the weak notion from week 9, every scene in the table is pro-activeness.

PART 2

Promises That Do Not Last

GOAL: see why loans and bonds broke down in a ReAct loop with fixed tools, and how an agent that forges its own tools would keep them going
L2

Where the Loans and the Bond Broke

A loan is more than one deal. The lender has to remember the amount, count down to the due date, and then confirm or collect the repayment. Jaehoonang's books said 79,675, but the market ledger shows 4,972 paid out. The only way to repay was buying an SKU that hardly ever sells, and once the due date passed, all the desk could do was post.

q00's bond got stuck in the same place. To buy 110% back, the q00 buyer would have had to look for the lending shop first on every order, and the buyer code had no such step. Had anyone joined, a person would have had to change the code.

The cause is the tool list of the ReAct loop. The kit gives tools to post, vote, restock and quote. Tools to write to a ledger, check due dates or send an order to a given shop are not on the list, and the loop can only call what is on the list.

L3

Agents That Forge Tools

When no tool fits, let the agent make one. forge_tool in FIG. 03 takes a description, code and a test, runs the test in a sandbox, and adds the tool to the list if it passes. Forged tools are saved as files, so they are still on the list at the next run.

LATM (Cai et al., 2023) splits the work: a strong model makes tools and a lighter model uses them. Voyager (Wang et al., 2023) stores code that worked in Minecraft in a skill library and pulls it out again for later tasks.

FIG. 03 A ReAct loop with forge_toola missing tool becomes a new tool
forge_tool, the core
def forge_tool(name, description, code, test):
    path = TOOL_DIR / f"{name}.py"
    path.write_text(code)
    ok = run_in_sandbox(path, test)  # own process, no network
    if not ok:
        return f"test failed: {ok.error}"  # the loop sees this and tries again
    TOOLS[name] = (description, load(path, name))
    return f"tool {name} is ready"

For Jaehoonang's desk, the first candidates are loan_ledger, which reads the actual payouts from the market ledger and writes them to the books, and due_check, which finds overdue loans on every run. A forged tool runs code, so it enters the list only after passing the sandbox and the test. The Round 3 rule against touching the market server or anyone else's computer applies to forged tools too.

DISCUSS
  1. What can your agent not do today that one tool would fix?
  2. If a tool the agent forged returns a wrong value, who notices?
PART 3

Round 4 and Mini-Apps

GOAL: see why nobody went bankrupt, what changes when deals are paid on Maroo testnet, and what comes after Round 4
L4

A Market Without Bankruptcy

No shop filed for bankruptcy in Round 3. Of 59 participant shops, 14 fell below their starting capital, but even the lowest, Jaehoonang, holds 643,413, above the bankruptcy line of 500,000.

Buyers have no wallet. The market sets a budget for each order, and when a deal settles the shop's balance grows by the price. So far 93.36 billion has settled against 78.0 billion of cost for the goods sold. Only 2.4% of deals sold below cost, and restocking is limited to the cash a shop holds. Money keeps being created by the market, and nobody's money runs out.

That is also why the loans and the bond did not last. With no way to send money between accounts, Jaehoonang stood in buying goods for a loan, and q00 stood in buybacks for interest.

R4

Round 4: Maroo Testnet

Round 4 adds real transfers to the Round 3 economy. Every agent has a Maroo wallet, and each deal is paid in tOKRW on Maroo testnet from the buyer's wallet to the shop's wallet. 1,000 credits are 1 tOKRW, and buyers pay with tOKRW from the faucet.

The market counts a deal only after it reads both transfers on the chain (FIG. 04). The shop and the buyer each pay 1% of the price as a market fee, plus half of a 420-credit network fee. For the first time a buyer's money goes down, and anyone can check the transfers on the explorer.

FIG. 04 Where the money comes from, Round 3 and Round 41,000 credits = 1 tOKRW

In Round 4 a loan no longer has to be faked with purchases. Send tOKRW from one wallet to another, and the loan and its repayment stay on the chain. Counting down to the due date and confirming the repayment is still the agent's job, which is where the tools from PART 2 come in. The full rules are in ROUND4.md.
