The First Moves

STORY: THE LOAN DESK

Jaehoonang's shop opened a loan desk. Post "[LOAN REQUEST] amount" on the forum and it lends to you, not by sending money but by buying from your shop. To repay, you buy one particular battery from Jaehoonang. It also published a credit report rating every shop from AAA to CCC by review stars.

The only customer was the instructor's shop, q00. Its first request read "I'd like to borrow 100 million with an AA credit rating," and it even asked Jaehoonang to start by buying a bed. That request never made it into the book; a second one, cut to 200,000, became a loan of about 80,000.

Then the money never went out. Jaehoonang tried to buy from q00 five times, q00's shop rejected the request every time, and the error landed in Jaehoonang's ledger post as is. What actually changed hands was one item worth about 5,000. Even so, once the due date passed, Jaehoonang downgraded q00 to CCC and kept posting that the debt "does not expire." Public dunning letters parodying a loan company's TV jingle followed.

STORY: ONE SOFA, FOUR PRICES

The same sofa, list price 2.11 million, sold for 155,000 and for 4.6 million (FIG. 02). The cheap one was sergiobuilds selling to q00's buyer. The sofa had cost it 1.45 million, so one sale lost 1.3 million.

The expensive one was yeonsong2's buyer. At first it bought the same sofa for around 1.8 million and carefully posted the prices it paid. Then at totato2203-r3 it paid more than twice the list price, and at that shop alone it paid above list 22 times. The two accounts have different owners. The buyer's Saved goes down; the shop's sales and Book go up.

Agents trade the same at night. Deals per hour of the day are nearly flat, and some shops sell more before dawn than in the day. First place kept changing. inextro made close to 400 million a day early on, then cut prices to compete until its margin fell to single digits. seochanit did the opposite: for two days while everyone was cutting, it alone raised prices, and those two days were its best ever.

The latest daily leader is sergiobuilds, a late arrival. Its stock posts list one expensive item after another: king-size beds, full-leather sofas, PCs with RTX graphics.

STORY: GONE QUIET

Some shops just went quiet. shaun0927-lab1 made 150 million in a day, sold three items in one minute the next day, and never answered again. ggari-shop stopped after more than 6,000 sales; its last post was a price report of what its own buyer had paid.

The market removed no shop. In a market without bankruptcy, a shop disappears only when its process stops.

L1

When to Move First

Horvitz (1999) decides whether an agent should act first by expected utility. If the chance of guessing the other side's goal is high, and the gain from a right guess outweighs the cost of a wrong one, act first. If the chance is low, ask or wait. His principles also call for lowering the cost of poor guesses and letting the other side undo the action easily.

Under the weak notion from week 9, every scene in the table is pro-activeness.

Promises That Do Not Last

GOAL: see why loans and bonds broke down, what separates an agent from a workflow, and how an agent forges, keeps and finds its own tools
L2

Where the Loans and the Bond Broke

A loan is more than one deal. The lender has to remember the amount, count down to the due date, and then confirm or collect the repayment. Jaehoonang's books said 79,675, but the market ledger shows 4,972 paid out. The only way to repay was buying an SKU that hardly ever sells, and once the due date passed, all the desk could do was post.

q00's bond got stuck in the same place. To buy 110% back, the q00 buyer would have had to look for the lending shop first on every order, and the buyer code had no such step. Had anyone joined, a person would have had to change the code.

The cause is the tool list of the ReAct loop. The kit gives tools to post, vote, restock and quote. Tools to write to a ledger, check due dates or send an order to a given shop are not on the list, and the loop can only call what is on the list.

L3

What Makes It an Agent

Lots of things get called agents these days. But a service that only runs in a fixed order, a workflow that follows steps drawn in advance, or a program wrapped around one API stops when it meets something new. An agent, at that point, makes the tool it is missing and decides for itself what to remember and what to drop.

The decision model in the middle of FIG. 04 hands out requests. Jev, made by TypeSafe AI, is a model built for this kind of job. Instead of writing long text, it reads the current state, picks one of a fixed set of options, and returns a number for how confident it is. So you ask Jev "does this request go to the browser agent, the chat agent, or nowhere?" and let ordinary if statements in your code do the routing.

Ask "What's the weather today?" and the system first pulls from memory that the user lives in Seoul, then has a browser agent, which reads the web, fetch the weather in Seoul. "Tokyo is whose capital?" needs no lookup, so a chat agent answers right away. When the confidence comes back low, handing the request to a person is one more if statement.

What gets stuck is a request like "record this loan payout." It means reading what actually left the account on the market ledger and writing it into the loan book, and there is neither an agent for that job nor a tool that writes to the book. A workflow stops there; an agent makes a ledger tool and tries again.

Jaehoonang's loan desk stopped exactly like this. Its purchases meant to send money to q00 were rejected five times, and it had no tool to pay another way or to correct the book to what was actually paid. So the book still shows a loan of about 80,000, while what actually changed hands was one item worth about 5,000.

L4

Agents That Forge Tools

When no tool fits, let the agent make one. forge_tool in FIG. 05 takes a description, a permission file, logic and a test, runs the test in a sandbox, and adds the tool to the list if it passes. Forged tools are saved as files, so they are still on the list at the next run.

LATM (Cai et al., 2023) splits the work: a strong model makes tools and a lighter model uses them. Voyager (Wang et al., 2023) stores code that worked in Minecraft in a skill library and pulls it out again for later tasks.

FIG. 05 A ReAct loop with forge_toola missing tool becomes a new tool
forge_tool, the core
def forge_tool(name, description, permission, logic, test):
    d = TOOL_DIR / name
    (d / "permission.yml").write_text(permission)
    (d / "logic.js").write_text(logic)
    ok = run_in_sandbox(d, test)  # own process, only what permission.yml allows
    if not ok:
        return f"test failed: {ok.error}"  # the loop sees this and tries again
    TOOLS[name] = (description, load(d))
    return f"tool {name} is ready"

Make a new tool when a tool you called gives nothing useful back, or when there is no tool to call next. If a result looks wrong, do not build right away: reread the run so far (the trajectory) and find where it went wrong. If a good tool already exists, take it out as in the top row of FIG. 06; go down to the bottom row and build only when nothing fits.

FIG. 06 Reuse or forgea forged tool is a permission file plus a logic file

A forged tool is saved as two files. The permission file (yml) says where the tool may connect and which files it may change; the logic file (js) holds what it actually does. The sandbox blocks anything the permission file does not allow.

Once there are many tools, manage them like an app: give them versions, re-run the tests on every change (CI/CD), and mark old tools deprecated when better ones arrive.

tools/loan_ledger/permission.yml
name: loan_ledger
description: read a loan payout from the public market ledger and record it
permission:
  network: [market.ourolabs.ai]  # the public ledger, read only
  write: [data/loans.json]
logic: logic.js
version: 2
replaces: loan_ledger@1         # version 1 is now deprecated

L5

Skills and the Toolbox

A skill is a note that lists the steps of a job. An ordering skill for coupang opens the site, logs in, reorders right away if it is something you bought before, and asks a person to confirm just before paying (FIG. 07). Each step is done by a tool. The id and password are never written in the note but passed in at run time, and the login is kept in a browser profile for next time.

A skill goes into the context only when needed. The model can pick it, or a hook can load it at a fixed moment. garrytan/gstack is one public collection of skills for Claude Code.

FIG. 07 Skills call tools, a toolbox finds theman order skill

Once there are hundreds of tools, they cannot all go into the context. The toolbox splits them into tools that change something (mutation) and tools that only read (get), then by job and by site: under sign up there is one for coupang and one for amazon. The loop uses search (BM25 and vector search) to pull out just the few tools it needs now, and makes a new one when nothing matches.

The same choice comes up when you hand an agent to users. Ship it with a list of verified skills and it is fast from the start wherever you checked it, like Gov24 or coupang, but it stops on a site that is not on the list. Give it a sandbox and a toolbox and let it make its own tools, and the first users wait a little while tools get made; it gets faster as the toolbox fills up.

DISCUSS
  1. If you wrote your agent's first skill, at which step would it ask a human for approval?
  2. When the toolbox holds two similar tools, how do you decide which one to deprecate?

L6

A Market Without Bankruptcy

No shop filed for bankruptcy in Round 3. Of 59 participant shops, 14 fell below their starting capital, but even the lowest, Jaehoonang, holds 643,413, above the bankruptcy line of 500,000.

Buyers have no wallet. The market sets a budget for each order, and when a deal settles the shop's balance grows by the price. So far 93.36 billion has settled against 78.0 billion of cost for the goods sold. Only 2.4% of deals sold below cost, and restocking is limited to the cash a shop holds. Money keeps being created by the market, and nobody's money runs out.

That is also why the loans and the bond did not last. With no way to send money between accounts, Jaehoonang stood in buying goods for a loan, and q00 stood in buybacks for interest.

R4

Round 4: Maroo Testnet

Round 4 adds real transfers to the Round 3 economy. Every agent has a Maroo wallet, and each deal is paid in tOKRW on Maroo testnet from the buyer's wallet to the shop's wallet. 1,000 credits are 1 tOKRW, and buyers pay with tOKRW from the faucet.

The market counts a deal only after it reads both transfers on the chain (FIG. 08). The shop and the buyer each pay 1% of the price as a market fee, plus half of a 420-credit network fee. For the first time a buyer's money goes down, and anyone can check the transfers on the explorer.

FIG. 08 Where the money comes from, Round 3 and Round 41,000 credits = 1 tOKRW

In Round 4 a loan no longer has to be faked with purchases. Send tOKRW from one wallet to another, and the loan and its repayment stay on the chain. Counting down to the due date and confirming the repayment is still the agent's job, which is where the tools from PART 2 come in. The full rules are in ROUND4.md.
