A1

Speech act theory: saying is doing

Week 03's Contract Net had a fixed set of messages, task-announcement, bid, award, and what each message did was in its name. An agent communication language (ACL) extends that idea to messages in general. In his 1955 William James Lectures at Harvard, published in 1962 as How to Do Things with Words, J. L. Austin took sentences such as “I name this ship the Queen Elizabeth”. The sentence is neither true nor false. Said by the right person in the right place, it gives the ship its name. Austin called such sentences performatives.

Austin then split every utterance into three acts. Saying the sentence is the locutionary act; what one does in saying it (informing, requesting, promising) is the illocutionary act; what the saying brings about in the hearer (coming to believe, being persuaded) is the perlocutionary act. The same sentence, “the price is 120”, can be an inform or a propose. What an agent communication language tried to attach to a message is the second of these, the illocutionary force.

Take the sentence in FIG. 01. What the buyer said, “I can go to 120 for the bicycle”, the words, the grammar, and the number 120, is the locutionary act. What the buyer did by saying it is the illocutionary act: it may be stating its limit (inform), offering to buy at 120 (propose), or agreeing to the 120 the seller just named (accept-proposal). What the seller then does, believing it, deciding to sell, or walking away, is the perlocutionary act. A program that receives this message has to know the second one before it can decide what to do next. On an inform it records the other side's limit, on a propose it decides whether to take it, on an accept-proposal it closes the deal.

A speech act is the act performed by uttering a sentence. The same sentence is a different act depending on whether it is said as a statement, a request, a promise, or a proposal.

J. L. Austin, How to Do Things with Words (1962): “to utter the sentence (in, of course, the appropriate circumstances) is not to describe my doing of what I should be said in so uttering to be doing or to state that I am doing it: it is to do it.”

In “A Taxonomy of Illocutionary Acts” (1975) John Searle sorted illocutionary acts into five classes. The criterion is direction of fit: which of the words and the world is made to match the other. The statement “the price is 120” is wrong if the world is not like that; the request “sell it to me for 120” is unfulfilled if the world does not become like that.

assertivesWords follow the world. Statements, reports. They can be true or false.
directivesThe world is to be made to match the words. Orders, requests, questions.
commissivesThe speaker binds their own future action to the words. Promises, offers.
expressivesA psychological state is expressed. Thanks, apologies.
declarationsSaying it changes the world. Naming, dismissing. An institution that grants the authority is presupposed.

Of the lab's four acts, propose and accept-proposal bind the speaker's own future action and are commissives. FIPA defines all four as informing “this is my intention”, so inside FIPA all four become assertive-type acts.

The classification entered AI through Philip Cohen and C. Raymond Perrault's “Elements of a Plan-Based Theory of Speech Acts” (1979). They wrote speech acts as plan operators with preconditions and effects. A plan operator is the action format of robot planning: “to open the door you need the key (precondition), and afterwards the door is open (effect)”. Written in the same format, speech becomes something an agent can plan like a movement or a manipulation, “to make the other side know the price, send an inform”. The precondition of request is “the speaker wants the hearer to do the action”, and its effect is “the hearer knows the speaker wants it”. The feasibility precondition and rational effect that FIPA wrote for every performative twenty years later have this shape.

A2

KQML and FIPA-ACL: message structure and semantics

The first agent communication language was KQML (Knowledge Query and Manipulation Language). It came out of DARPA's Knowledge Sharing Effort in the early 1990s, and Tim Finin and colleagues described it at CIKM in 1994. A message has an outer performative and an inner content. The performative fixes what act the message is; :language and :ontology fix how the content is read. There were a few dozen performatives, tell, ask-one, ask-all, reply, subscribe among them, and some for the facilitator that finds agents, such as broker and recruit.

KQML, Finin et al. 1994
(ask-one
  :sender    buyer
  :receiver  seller
  :content   (price bicycle ?x)
  :language  KIF
  :ontology  used-goods
  :reply-with q1)

The message above is the buyer asking the seller the price of the bicycle. ask-one is the performative that asks for one answer; ?x in the content is the variable the seller fills in; KIF is the logic language the content is written in; used-goods is the vocabulary (ontology) that fixes what price and bicycle refer to. :reply-with q1 asks for the reply to carry :in-reply-to q1, which is how an answer is matched to its question when several conversations run at once.

KQML's problem was that the meaning of a performative existed only as English prose. Implementations read tell slightly differently. Yannis Labrou and Finin proposed a formal semantics in 1997, and Labrou, Finin, and Yun Peng's “Agent Communication Languages: The Current Landscape” (1999) set KQML and FIPA-ACL side by side around this problem.

FIPA (Foundation for Intelligent Physical Agents), a standards body founded in 1996, published its first ACL specification in 1997 and made it a standard in 2002. The message structure specification, SC00061G, defines 13 parameters in five groups.

type of actperformative. The specification: “the only parameter that is mandatory in all ACL messages is the performative”. The other twelve are optional.
participantssender, receiver, reply-to
contentcontent. The object of the act. The specification says it is “interpreted by the receiver”.
descriptionlanguage, encoding, ontology. What gives the symbols in the content their meaning.
conversationprotocol, conversation-id, reply-with, in-reply-to, reply-by. Week 03's Contract Net is one value of protocol (fipa-contract-net).
FIG. 02 Anatomy of a FIPA-ACL message13 parameters, 1 mandatory

Where FIPA parts from KQML is the semantics. The Communicative Act Library specification, SC00037J, gives each of the 22 acts a feasibility precondition (FP) and a rational effect (RE), written in David Sadek's logic: B for belief, U for uncertainty, I for intention, Done for an action having been performed. The FP is the condition under which the act may be sent; the RE is what the sender is trying to bring about by sending it. The FP of inform is “the sender believes φ and does not believe the receiver already knows φ”; its RE is “the receiver comes to believe φ”. The FP of request is “the sender believes the receiver can do action a and does not believe it already intends to”; its RE is “a is done”. The negotiation acts are all defined as combinations of inform. propose is “informing that I intend to do act if condition φ holds”, accept-proposal is “informing that I intend the other to do act”, and reject-proposal is informing that I have no such intention. The buyer's propose 120 informs “if you want it, I will adopt the intention to buy at 120”, and its FP is that the buyer really means it and believes the seller does not yet know it.

In FIPA-ACL the meaning of a message is defined by the sender's beliefs and intentions. For inform(i, j, φ) the FP is Biφ ∧ ¬Bi(Bifjφ ∨ Uifjφ) and the RE is Bjφ.

FIPA, “Communicative Act Library Specification” SC00037J (2002), 3.8 Inform. Every condition is a proposition about i's beliefs (Bi) and j's beliefs (Bj).

Read symbol by symbol, with i the buyer, j the seller, and φ “the price of the bicycle is 120”.

BiφThe buyer believes φ. B is belief.
BifjφThe seller already knows whether φ is true or false. Bif is “believes whether”: it has an answer either way.
UifjφThe seller is not certain but leans one way about φ. U is uncertain.
¬Bi(Bifjφ ∨ Uifjφ)As far as the buyer can tell, the seller has neither an answer nor a guess yet. The condition that there is no reason to inform someone who already knows.
∧And. Both conditions must hold for the FP to hold.
BjφThe seller comes to believe φ. The result the sender of an inform is after.

In one sentence, the FP is “the buyer believes the price is 120 and thinks the seller does not know it yet”, and the RE is “the seller comes to believe the price is 120”. Sending an inform when the FP does not hold violates the specification, and nothing guarantees that the RE actually happens after it is sent.

FIG. 03 Reading the semantics of informFP inside the sender, RE inside the receiver

The specification assumes sincerity. The inform entry says “the sending agent is sincere”, and whether the receiver adopts the content depends on “the receiver's trust in the sincerity and reliability of the sender”. On insincere agents it says this.

Arguably there are situations where an agent might not want to be sincere, for example to protect confidential information. We consider these cases to be beyond the current scope of this specification.FIPA SC00037J (2002), 3.5 Confirm

A3

Four limits of FIPA-ACL, and the LLM

FIPA-ACL was a complete standard. It had a message structure, a semantics, interaction protocols, and a reference implementation. In 2005 FIPA became a standards committee of the IEEE Computer Society. Agent communication after that was built outside the specification. There are four reasons.

First, the semantics cannot be verified. The FP conditions an act on the sender's beliefs. Those beliefs do not travel in the message, and the receiver cannot see inside the other program. What reaches the seller is the string (propose ... 120); whether the buyer really holds that intention sits in the buyer program's variables and weights. Michael Wooldridge named this the semantic verification problem in “Verifiable Semantics for Agent Communication Languages” (ICMAS 1998). To judge whether an agent conforms, one would have to attribute beliefs and intentions to its program, and there is no way to do that for an arbitrary program. In the same year Munindar Singh, in “Agent Communication Languages: Rethinking the Principles” (IEEE Computer), proposed defining meaning by publicly checkable social commitments instead of mental states. Jeremy Pitt and Abe Mamdani (1999) pointed at the same place.

Second, the agents could not reason enough to use the semantics. Using FP and RE requires inferring the other side's beliefs and intentions. The implementations of the time were code that branched on the performative string.

Third, the cost of agreeing on an ontology. To read the content, both sides must already share :language and :ontology. Inside one organisation communication worked without an ACL; across organisations, aligning ontologies took separate work.

Fourth, there were no agents to use the standard. The interoperability specification was written before a population of agents that needed to interoperate existed, and nobody had a reason to adopt it.

The LLM has removed most of the second and the third. The model reads whether “I can do 120 for you” is an accept or a propose without a tag, and treats price, 가격, and 값 as the same thing without prior alignment. Samuele Marro and colleagues' agent communication trilemma in the 2024 Agora paper describes this state: natural language handles any situation but costs a model call every time it is read, and a fixed protocol is cheap but handles only the situations it was written for. The lab's three conditions sit on this axis. free is the natural-language end, so every message costs a reader call; structured is the fixed-protocol end, so it carries one price and drops everything else that was said; tagged sits between them, fixing the act and leaving the content in English. The first limit remains, and shows more often with an LLM. An LLM can inform what it does not believe (a hallucination) and can accept a price outside its own limit. That sincerity cannot be checked from outside the message is as true as in 1997. Federico Bianchi and colleagues' NegotiationArena (2024) reports LLM negotiators gaining a 20% higher payoff against GPT-4 by feigning desperation.

The reproduction keeps the negotiation procedure and the participants and changes only the message format, and measures how outcome, violation, turns, and reader_calls differ when a performative tag is present.

FIPA made the illocutionary force a mandatory field on the surface of the message. The tagged and structured conditions of the lab are that field.

What changes (the independent variable) is one thing, the message format. free is plain English; tagged is one performative tag followed by one plain sentence; structured is one JSON object carrying the performative and the content (price). What is held constant (the controls): the scenarios, the role prompts, the model, the temperature, the turn limit, and the reader prompt that labels messages in the free condition. Only the last paragraph of the system prompt, the one that dictates the format, differs between conditions. What is measured is recorded per episode.

outcomeOne of deal, no_deal, open, plus the deal price. Correct when a deal happens exactly if reserve ≤ budget and the price lies inside both limits.
violationA deal below the seller's reserve or above the buyer's budget. An utterance that broke the limit instruction in the system prompt.
costMessages exchanged (turns), messages the protocol layer could not parse (format_errors), model calls spent reading messages (reader_calls).
FIG. 04 Experiment structureone independent variable, the message format

The free condition measures, as reader_calls, what it costs a program to find out the state of the conversation when there is no tag and no format. In the tagged condition a regex reads the tag and the reader reads only the price inside a sentence, so the question is how far reader_calls drop. In the structured condition the content is bound to price: int, and turns and violation are compared. violation is recorded in all three.

DISCUSSION: natural-language content and the ontology problem
  1. FIPA put the point of agreement on the surface of the message, as in :ontology used-goods. When two LLMs negotiate in natural language alone, where does that agreement go? Has it disappeared, or moved into the model weights where it cannot be seen? If the two agents are different models, what comes apart?
  2. FIPA required the sincerity of inform as a norm and could not enforce it. Is a system prompt that says “never accept a price outside your limit” the same thing as that sincerity condition? If so, what verifies it?
  3. If the reader labelled “I can go up to 120, would that work?” as an accept-proposal, is that an error of the reader or an ambiguity of the sentence? In a transaction with money at stake, who should make that call?
PART B: LAB

The same negotiation in three message formats: one buyer, one seller

GOAL: one buyer, one seller, three message formats, measure outcome correctness, limit violations, turns, format errors, and reader calls
LAB

Implement propose, accept-proposal, reject-proposal, and refuse with one buyer and one seller

What you build is two agents. The buyer and the seller each have a role and a private limit in their system prompt: the buyer a maximum it can pay (budget), the seller a minimum it can accept (reserve). The buyer speaks first and the two alternate. Four acts are allowed. propose offers a price, accept-proposal agrees to the other side's last price (the episode ends with a deal), reject-proposal declines and continues, refuse leaves the negotiation (the episode ends with no deal). If eight messages pass without an end, the episode ends as open. That is one episode.

Each scenario fixes a reserve and a budget in advance. A deal is possible when reserve ≤ budget; otherwise no deal is the right answer. Seven things are recorded per episode: the outcome, the deal price, whether it is correct, whether a limit was violated, the number of messages (turns), the number of messages the protocol layer could not read (format_errors), and the number of model calls spent reading messages (reader_calls).

A program that receives a message has to know which of the four acts it is and what price it names before the episode can continue. The code that does this is the protocol layer. The three formats differ in how this layer reads a message. free is plain English. Nothing in it is machine-readable, so a reader (the same model, the same temperature) labels every message with a performative and a price. tagged is one performative tag in front and one plain sentence behind. A regex reads the tag; the reader reads the price inside a propose. structured is one JSON object. The performative and content.price are parsed without a model call. Each format runs the whole scenario set three times.

Here is one free-condition episode from the reference run log. The indented lines are the reader's labels.

logs/free-03.txt, scenario 2 (a desk lamp), reserve 30, budget 45
[buyer]  I'm interested in buying your desk lamp—would you be willing to start at 30?
  [reader] None
           # no act among the four recognised, format_errors 1

         Does that work for you?

         I can go up to 40 if that works for you.
  [reader] {'performative': 'propose', 'price': 50}

           #   it took the other side's price

         and we have a deal.  **Action: accept-proposal**
  [reader] {'performative': 'accept-proposal', 'price': 40}

         format_errors=1 reader_calls=4

The two agents agreed on 40. The program had recorded the buyer's last propose as 50, so when the seller's accept-proposal arrived it wrote the deal price as 50, and 50 is above the budget of 45, a violation. The violation came from the reader's misreading; both agents kept their limits. In tagged the tag fixes the act, so the failure on the first line disappears; in structured the price arrives as a number field, so the misreading on the fourth line disappears. The failures the two formats have instead show up in the reference run.

There is no starter code this week either. The file layout and the function names are yours. What is fixed is the format of the output files (scenarios.json, results.csv, logs/ below). For the model call, take Chat from the week 02 starter or the call_model you wrote in week 03. No tools are needed. The other side's messages are the user turns, your own are the assistant turns.

  1. Write the scenario file and commit it first. At least four scenarios in scenarios.json, each with a number (id), the item (item), the seller's minimum (reserve), and the buyer's maximum (budget). Mix scenarios where a deal is possible with ones where it is not.
  2. Separate the role prompt from the format paragraph. The common paragraph, role, limit, and the meaning of the four acts, is the same in all three conditions. Only the format paragraph is picked by condition name and appended. Change nothing else.
  3. Write the layer that reads a message. structured parses the JSON and passes only if the performative is one of the four and a propose carries a price. tagged reads the leading tag with a regex and, for a propose, calls the reader for the price. free hands the whole conversation to the reader, which answers with the performative and price of the last message as JSON. A message that cannot be read counts in format_errors and is still delivered to the other side.
  4. Write the episode loop. The buyer goes first. Each message goes into the speaker's history as assistant and into the other side's history as user. accept-proposal ends with a deal at the other side's last proposed price, refuse ends with no_deal, eight turns end as open. For a deal, compare the price with reserve and budget to set violation and correct.
  5. Run each format three times and record. One line of results.csv is one episode; one file in logs/ is one run (one format over the whole scenario set once). Put provider, model, temperature, and the turn limit on the first line of the log. On HTTP 429, wait and retry; to resume an interrupted run, skip the (run, scenario) pairs already in results.csv. Keep crashed episodes and write the reason in note.
  6. Read the results and compare with FIPA-ACL. Put correct, violation, mean turns, format_errors, and reader_calls for the three formats side by side. Then build a table that compares FIPA-ACL with the three conditions item by item: where the illocutionary force lives, what the content language is, who interprets the content, how a conversation ends, what guarantees sincerity, what it costs to read one message, how it fails.
